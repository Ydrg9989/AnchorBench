Anchoring---the tendency for numeric estimates to shift toward
contextually salient reference points---is widely documented in
both human cognition and large language models (LLMs), yet
existing LLM evaluations treat anchoring monolithically: they
test a single delivery mechanism, conflate sensitivity to
irrelevant numbers with rational use of informative context,
and lack grounding in the distinct psychological paradigms that
produce anchoring in humans.
We introduce \textsc{AnchorBench}, a theory-grounded benchmark
that maps four established human anchoring paradigms---classic
external anchoring, self-generated anchoring, numeric priming,
and ecological anchoring---to five LLM interface channels
(prompt-embedded, conversation history, in-context learning,
retrieval-augmented generation, and tool-call outputs).
Crucially, the benchmark introduces an explicit
\emph{anchor-relevance axis} that distinguishes transparently
irrelevant anchors from plausibly informative ones, enabling
measurement of both raw susceptibility and relevance
discrimination---a reliability dimension invisible to standard
accuracy metrics.
Pilot studies on all five suites (external, history, ICL, RAG,
and tool) across four models validate the design using a unified
evaluation framework.
The Unified Anchor Influence (UAI) metric---which measures
the fraction of available shift toward the anchor that
is realized---enables direct comparison across suites
with different anchor-delivery mechanisms.
Results show that anchoring susceptibility is
paradigm-dependent: model rankings on both task accuracy
and discrimination reverse across suites, and no model
is consistently best or worst.
Plausible anchors produce stronger influence than irrelevant
ones in 18 of 20 model--suite combinations, but the
magnitude and consistency of discrimination vary
substantially, confirming the value of multi-paradigm
evaluation.
